I've kept a diary since I was 5, so I've been running a 30 year experiment on journaling and note-taking. As of 2026, this is what I've determined: *There is no tool more well-suited for the task of taking notes than the pen and paper.*

This post will be more about just taking notes; rather I'll be writing more generally about the advantage of pen and paper in certain domains, and how I prefer to augment those domains with digital tools.

## An Argument for Carrying Always Carrying Pen and Paper

My first Google Keep note was taken on July 2, 2016. It says "Dog trained to sniff out stink." I don't know what that means, perhaps a business idea. Before that, I was keeping one-off notes in whatever the native notekeeping app was for any given phone I have, the records of which I no longer have. My point is, the most obvious on-the-go notetaking device for quick thoughts is our phone, and it's a pretty good tool: if you take your note, and fall into a river 5 minutes later, your note will be safe in the cloud. You can persist your note for a decade, like I've done for Google Keep, and search and categorize your notes. 

However, Google Keep is not really a great organizational tool. You can't link notes to notes, your formatting options are limited, as are your attachment options. You can't have a list and regular text in the same note. The searching is somewhat slow, you can't download all notes as a text file, and if you want to view you first written note, the only way to do is is spend 8 minutes scrolling down while watching a Youtube video, as I've just now done. Realistically, you'd want to jot down a quick note in Google Keep, so as to extract it later to put into a more robust digital system; I'll describe such systems later.

Rather than use this halfway-tool, I argue instead for carrying a small notepad and a pen. For me, that's usually a [Maruman Mnemosyne N192](https://www.jetpens.com/Maruman-Mnemosyne-N192A-Twin-Ring-Memo-Pad-Modified-B7-5-mm-Lined/pd/11412) or a [Kokuyo soft wring memo pad](https://www.jetpens.com/KOKUYO-Soft-Ring-Memo-Pad-A7-Modified-Graph-Black/pd/24972), but a general theme of all my arguments here is that it really doesn't matter, one of the advantages of pen and paper is you don't need to go down a rabbit hole of tooling like when choosing software; literally any pen, and any paper, will do.

Why? Consider the scenarios you jot down notes in your phone while you're out and about. One that happens to me quite often: someone is telling me about something I want to look into later. Maybe it's an LLM tool, a web framework, a book, a movie, an accounting method, whatever it is you talk about with people. In my experience, when you whip out the notebook and pen, flip the cover, and start jotting down while they're talking to you, it has a kinda hilariously huge impact. They think, "damn, he REALLY cares about what I'm telling him." It's an indicator of respect: what you're telling me is so important, I'm going to put ink to paper about it. 

Contrast with the digital method: you take out your phone, open Keep, and jot it down there. The person across from you will probably assume you're taking a note, and you'll probably have told them as such so they don't think you're checking your messages real quick mid-conversation, which would of course be rude. That doesn't matter, because they perceive you as doing the "checking my messages" action, and some part of them will wonder if they have your full attention anymore. That small part might be right. As soon as you opened your phone, several trillion-dollar companies are immediately fighting for your attention, and their psychological-interface designers are more than capable of overcoming your pea-brained, not-trillion-dollar attention span. Next thing you know, you're replying to an Instagram message mid-conversation. Not a good look.

The pen and paper can't distract you: it's a singular tool with a singular purpose. You take it out, record your thought, and put it away. There's a certain tactile satisfaction that comes with the maneuver, and you can't help but feel a little bit like a gumshoe detective in a noir. A far healthier experience than a phone which is full of apps designed to make you feel small, angry, and tired. 

If you're like me, you may be using a more advanced system than just chucking things in Google Keep. You may have the Orgzly app syncthing'd to your org folder which is shared across all devices and has an org-agenda system that you spent three years tweaking, and a todo setup that's organized along Getting Things Done principles. Rather than having to deal with scraps of paper or Google Keep notes, you always put your notes straight into that system, so everything is organized, or at least processed, in one place. Again, this is still a halfway solution. It's better organized than Google Keep, but not as functional as org (or whatever) on your PC, by nature of it being a mobile app that you use your thumbs to interact with. There's also an insurmountable dichtotomy: the more usable it is on your phone, the more friction it takes to get the note written. More friction means you're missing things your conversational partner is telling you; it means more time for your phone to get its hooks in you. 

Instead, write it on a piece of paper, and wait till you get home to put the note into your highly complicated and very well organized digital system, when it's easier to use that system anyway.


## Nothing is Faster than Pen and Paper

There are well-studied benefits to writing things out that by now are so well-known I'm not even going to bother citing them. Just google "benefits of handwriting" to read the myriad of studies showing how handwriting makes it more likely you'll remember something. With that in mind, I've tried many digital handwriting methods. In order of my experiments:

1. 2015: Microsoft Surface Pro, using the OneNote app. This was actually quite effective in my bootcamp. 
2. 2016: Lenovo Yoga. I wanted to only carry one device. I could put Linux on the Lenovo and use Xournal++ to take notes, albeit on a massive, heavy device.
3. 2018: Microsoft Surface Go. Again, desperate for some way to only ever carry one device, I thought I could maybe run linux in a VM on this, or use WSL. Hah, no. At least the notetaking experience in Onenote was still pretty good... until I wanted to export my notes.
4. 2022: Samsung Galaxy zfold 3: An attempt to only need to carry one device + a laptop. 
5. 2023: Boox Nova Air. I'd been marking up some of my paper books by hand, and really liked the experience. 90% of my books I read digitally, so I wanted to try marking up digital books. I also downloaded some calendar PDFs to use as a "paper calendar."
6. 2024: Boox Go 10inch. Essentially a cheaper version of the reMarkable, and often neck and neck in reviews. Certainly a better UX and exporting experience. The Nova Air writing experience wasn't great, but it was phenomenal on this. It is, imo, the best digital writing experience, and I was very much enjoying marking up my books, "paper calendar," and simple hand-written notes in this thing. 
7. 2025: Kobo Libra Color. For reasons I'll detail below, I'd given up entirely on digital notetaking, and just wanted a way to markup my thoughts on books as I read them. The Boox Go has no backlight and is massive, so wasn't serving me well as my take-everywhere ereader, so at this point I was at pen-and-paper plus this. 

Now I'm down to a Kobo Clara 2E, a Framework 13, and any and all handwriting is done on pen and paper only.

All of the above devices are only good for taking handwritten notes in one situation: you're at a desk, alone, on your own time. I thought I could whip out the Galaxy fold, pull the s-pen off the case, and bang out a note right as the vet started talking to me. Nope. I have to open the notetaking app, open a new note, oops it's like, not loading for some reason, wtf why? Force close the app, sorry one second lemme just note that down. I thought I could open my screen cover on the Boox Go and invoke the gesture I'd selected to go straight to a new note. Wait, the gesture didn't work, one sec, trying again, and again, ah damn that opened my previous read book, lemme try... nope. 

At the end of the day, nothing can beat the speed and reliability of opening a notebook to a blank page and putting a pen to it. You might even have a bookmark on it to make it even faster. Every meeting, therapy session, doctor appointment, brainstorming session, card game, I just take out my notebook, pull out my pen, and I'm ready to go. 

## Notes Processing

All of my previous solutions were still halfway solutions. At the end of the day, my notes jotted into a book aren't really useful to me in anyway. I can't search them, link them, or reference them. Same too for my typed notes. In between my experiments above, I was also doing experiments with typed notes, usually in some kind of org-mode system. No matter how portable the device, this was still slower and less reliable than pen and paper, for the same reasons written above. Furthermore, these systems suffer the same dichotomy as the app: it can be immediate, or it can fit into my overall digital organization system, but it can't be both, because for it to fit well into my digital organization system, it needs to be tagged, linked, and possibly put in a certain folder. So, org notes would typically go into a 'daily' file, or be org-captured somewhere. Like my digitally handwritten notes, or my Google Keep notes or whatever, they'd need to be **processed**.

I'm a pen and paper advocate, but I'm not a luddite (actually [I am](https://craphound.com/news/2022/01/10/science-fiction-is-a-luddite-literature/), but that's for another day): I maintained an awesome org-mode system for years, which I've now transfered to Trilium + a digital calendar. I interact with this system on a laptop or desktop, and it's quite feature-rich. I can link things, tag them, move them around, have them presented to me in custom views, sorted, filtered, and so on. All things absolutely impossible with a handwritten system, and quite clumsy to try to do on a phone (I've gone so far as to install emacs directly on my phone and try).

I say, no matter the system for note taking books or meetings, or jotting down thoughts as you're out and about, that system should simply be a jotting system, to be processed when you're at your computer. For me, that looks like either transcribing notes wholesale, recording the gist, creating todos, or creating calendar notes. Then, I chuck the notes into a jar on my desk that I empty once a year:

Given that I've back-loaded all the heavy lifting, why not make the input as dead-simple as possible? Pen and paper.

## The Pen and Paper TODO

My main TODO system used to exist in a frankly quite awesome org-agenda system, but I was [fiddling with it too much](/posts/my-new-life-stack/#task-management), so I switched to a simpler day to day system in Trilium. However, this system has that same dichotomy issue: there's friction to get TODOs into the system. So, I also use a system that I stole from an unknown Hacker News commenter, who I still can't find. Sorry, stranger. 

The system is simple: That aformentioned small notebook I carry around has my day's TODOs on the first page. The back pages contain random thoughts or notes from meetings or books. This means that when my partner tells me that she needs me to take the trash out before I leave, I can just quickly jot that down. I also tend to copy my day's TODOs from my main system into the notebook in the morning, so I can see all the day's TODOs in one place. If there are some TODOs in the notebook at the end of the day that, for whatever reason, aren't in my main system, I transcribe them in. In this way I also engage in a TODO pruning: I may move them to become week or month TODOs if I didn't get them done in a day, or throw them into an archive. Then, I rip out the day's page, and chuck it in my jar.

This system comes with all the speed and reliability benefits previously mentioned, but it also has a very fun tactile element to it wherein I cross out TODOs I complete. I used to scoff at these sorts of silly little motivational tricks, but one thing I've been learning as I get older is that I'm not special, I am just a sloppy-brained human, and that sloppy-brained human tricks work as well on me as anyone else. So, I don't fight these tricks anymore to make myself "immune" to them (which is at worst impossible and at best a waste of energy), I instead leverage these tricks as shortcuts. It feels good to scratch out the TODO, it gives me a little dopamine hit. I'll take those where I can get them.
