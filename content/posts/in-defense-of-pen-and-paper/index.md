I've kept a diary since I was 5, so I've been running a 30 year experiment on journaling and note-taking. As of 2026, this is what I've determined: **There is no tool more well-suited for the task of taking notes than pen and paper.**

This post will be about more than just taking notes; rather I'll be writing more generally about the advantage of pen and paper in certain domains, and how I prefer to augment those domains with digital tools. Also, don't think of me as some kind of pulp-hauling paper evangelist: I almost exclusively read on e-ink devices, and towards the end of this article I'll advocate for a digital system that functions as the "Authoritative Knowledge System" of any note intake method.

## It's Better than Your Phone

My first Google Keep note was taken on July 2, 2016. It says "Dog trained to sniff out stink." I don't know what that means, perhaps a bizarre business idea. Before that, I was keeping one-off notes in whatever the native note taking app was for any given phone I had, the records of which I no longer have. My point is, the most obvious on-the-go note taking device for quick thoughts is our phone, and it's a pretty good tool: if you take your note, and fall into a river 5 minutes later, your note will be safe in the cloud. You can persist your note for a decade, like I've done for Google Keep, and search and categorize your notes. 

However, Google Keep is not really a great organizational tool. You can't link notes to notes, your formatting options are limited, as are your attachment options. You can't have a list and regular text in the same note. The searching is somewhat slow, you can't download all notes as a text file, and if you want to view your first written note, the only way to do so is to spend 8 minutes scrolling down while watching a Youtube video, as I've just done now . Realistically, you'd want to jot down a quick note in Google Keep, so as to extract it later to put into a more robust digital system. I'll describe such systems later.

Rather than use this halfway-tool, I argue instead for carrying a small notepad and a pen. For me, that's usually a [Maruman Mnemosyne N192](https://www.jetpens.com/Maruman-Mnemosyne-N192A-Twin-Ring-Memo-Pad-Modified-B7-5-mm-Lined/pd/11412) or a [Kokuyo soft wring memo pad](https://www.jetpens.com/KOKUYO-Soft-Ring-Memo-Pad-A7-Modified-Graph-Black/pd/24972), and as a pen a small metal Pilot Frixion (which is great for its size, 5mm point, and because it's erasable), but it doesn't matter, one of the advantages of pen and paper is you don't need to go down a rabbit hole of tooling like when choosing software. Literally any pen, and any paper, will do.

Why choose this over a phone? Consider the scenarios that lead to you tapping out notes in your phone. One that happens to me quite often: someone is telling me about something I want to look into later. Maybe it's an LLM tool, a web framework, a book, a movie, an accounting method, whatever it is I talk about with people. In my experience, when I whip out the notebook and pen, flip the cover, and start jotting down while they're talking to me, it has a kinda hilariously huge impact. They think, "damn, he REALLY cares about what I'm telling him." It's an indicator of respect: what you're telling me is so important to me, I'm going to put ink to paper about it.

Contrast with the digital method: you take out your phone, open Keep, and tap it into a note there. The person across from you will probably assume you're taking a note, and you'll probably have told them as such so they don't think you're checking your messages real quick mid-conversation, which would of course be rude. That doesn't matter, because their monkey brain will perceive you as doing the "checking my messages" action, and some small part of them will wonder if they have your full attention anymore. That small part might be right. As soon as you open your phone, several trillion-dollar companies are immediately fighting for your attention, and their psycho-interface designers are more than capable of overcoming your pea-brained, not-trillion-dollar attention span. Next thing you know, you're replying to an Instagram message mid-conversation.

The pen and paper can't distract you: it's a singular tool with a singular purpose. You take it out, record your thought, and put it away. There's a certain tactile satisfaction that comes with the maneuver, and you can't help but feel a little bit like a gumshoe detective in a noir film. A far healthier experience than a phone, which is full of apps designed to make you feel small, angry, and tired.

If you're like me, you may be using a more advanced system than just chucking things in Google Keep. You may have the Orgzly app syncthing'd to your org folder which is shared across all devices and has an org-agenda system that you spent three years tweaking, and a todo setup that's organized along Getting Things Done principles. Rather than having to deal with scraps of paper or Google Keep notes, you always put your notes straight into that system, so everything is organized, or at least processed, in one place. Again, this is still a halfway solution. It's better organized than Google Keep, but not as functional as org (or whatever) on your PC, by nature of it being a mobile app that you use your thumbs to interact with. There's also an insurmountable dichotomy: the more usable it is on your phone, the more friction it takes to get the note written. More friction means you're missing things your conversational partner is telling you; it means more time for your phone to get its hooks in you.

Instead, write it on a piece of paper, and wait till you get home to put the note into your highly complicated and very well organized Authoritative Knowledge System, when it's easier to use that system anyway. This reduction of friction is huge. When I have a no-friction way to get down thoughts, I find myself jotting down a lot more little app ideas, blog ideas, story ideas, projects I want to try, or silly little questions I think of, than when I handled each of those things in their own app or system. Now instead of getting sucked into a rabbit hole on a question like "How come phones can have magnets near them when we used to not be able to put magnets near screens," alongside giving my phone more time to distract me, I can just compile a bunch into a single ChatGPT query, preferably on my computer which has less psychofauna on it to distract me: "Why can we put magnets near phones but not monitors before; who is the USA in debt to; how does DNS work in Taiwan if the PRC cuts all our cables; please answer each succinctly in a few sentences." Since I've started this system, I've realized that, before, I left a lot of thoughts unexplored. They'd simply be forgotten, or repeated in their simplest form every fifty showers or so. Now that I get these thoughts down, they get explored, developed, and sometimes turned into apps or blog posts or cool activities with friends.

I heard in a [Last Lecture](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uxoCnxlxpIk) an idea I've been kicking around lately: Friction is a killer of willpower, and willpower is a limited resource. I'm currently obsessed with reducing friction as much as possible.


## It's Better than Your reMarkable

There are well-studied benefits to writing things out by hand that by now are so well-known I'm not even going to bother citing them. Just google "benefits of handwriting" to read the myriad of studies showing how handwriting makes it more likely you'll remember something. With that in mind, I've tried many digital handwriting methods. In order of time:

1. 2015: Microsoft Surface Pro, using the OneNote app. This was actually quite effective in my bootcamp. 
2. 2016: Lenovo Yoga. I wanted to only carry one device. I could put Linux on the Lenovo and use Xournal++ to take notes, albeit on a massive, heavy device.
3. 2018: Microsoft Surface Go. Again, desperate for some way to only ever carry one device, I thought I could maybe run linux in a VM on this, or use WSL. Hah, no. At least the note taking experience in Onenote was still pretty good... until I wanted to export my notes.
4. 2022: Samsung Galaxy zfold 3: An attempt to only need to carry one device + a laptop. 
5. 2023: Boox Nova Air. I'd been marking up some of my paper books by hand, and really liked the experience. 90% of my books I read digitally, so I wanted to try marking up digital books. I also downloaded some calendar PDFs to use as a "paper calendar."
6. 2024: Boox Go. Essentially a cheaper version of the reMarkable, and often neck and neck in reviews. Certainly a better UX and exporting experience. The Nova Air writing experience wasn't great, hence why I changed to the Boox Go. It is, imo, the best digital writing experience, and I was very much enjoying marking up my books, "paper calendar," and simple hand-written notes in this thing. 
7. 2025: Kobo Libra Color. For reasons I'll detail below, at this point I'd given up entirely on digital note taking, and just wanted a way to markup my thoughts on books as I read them. The Boox Go has no backlight and is massive, so wasn't serving me well as my take-everywhere e-reader, so at this point I was at pen-and-paper plus this. 

Now I'm down to a Kobo Clara 2E, a Framework 13, and any and all handwriting is done on pen and paper only.

All of the above devices are only good for taking handwritten notes in one situation: you're at a desk, alone, on your own time. I thought I could whip out the Galaxy fold, pull the s-pen off the case, and bang out a note right as the vet started talking to me. Nope. I have to open the note taking app, open a new note, oops it's like, not loading for some reason, wtf why? Force close the app, sorry one second lemme just note that down. I thought I could open my screen cover on the Boox Go and invoke the gesture I'd selected to go straight to a new note. Wait, the gesture didn't work, one sec, trying again, and again, ah damn that opened my previous read book, lemme try... nope. 

Nothing can beat the speed and reliability of opening a notebook to a blank page and putting a pen to it. Every meeting, therapy session, doctor appointment, brainstorming session, card game, I just take out my notebook, pull out my pen, and I'm ready to go. With digital systems, there's issues with reliability: apps may crash, gestures may fail, you might forget your stylus or the tip may have fallen out, or you might have simply forgotten to charge the device. With digital devices, there's friction: you'll be tempted to interact with some kind of organization system, you'll want to engage your hyperoptimization tendency to create some kind of auto-convert-to-org-note-flow, you need to open the correct app, you need to create a new note. Depending on the digital device, you may be facing an onslaught of psychofauna trying to steal your attention (Boox devices are basically just Android tablets, for example). 

On top of all this, digital devices just aren't as good of a writing experience as pen and paper, and in my opinion never will be. reMarkable has been trying for, what, a decade now? They have very smart industrial designers, they've invested probably millions into solving the problem, and to this day people still complain about the minute microsecond of delay that's just barely noticeable, or the tiny few mm separation between where the tablet tip touches the screen and where the ink is rendered. Palm rejection is sometimes funky. Sometimes you accidentally input a gesture. Changing colors or pen sizes is never as simple as picking up a different pen or pencil. Getting a new page is never as fast or simple as flipping a real page. Going back and forth between pages, the device never renders as quickly as realtime, nor can you "peek" pages as easily. Not to mention,  price. 400$ plus a subscription for a worse UX than pen and paper, as well as a worse Authoritative Knowledge System UX than org-mode, Obsidian, Trilium, or whatever. Plus, you don't get the tactile satisfaction of filled notebooks on a shelf, or torn out slips of paper in a jar. Another halfway solution. Another solution with friction. 

## It's Better than Your E-Reader

Parallel to [all my experiments with digital paper devices](#its-better-than-your-remarkable), I was doing an experiment on the best way to take notes on books I read. Typically that's involved some sort of on-device highlighting feature, or scribbling in the margins with devices like the Boox Go or Kobo Libra Color. I've also tried reading next to a computer so I can type out my notes, which obviously doesn't work for the most common reading situation I find myself in that's outside the house: riding public transit. 

So, what's a flow for that look like? Usually, it's flipping back through my highlights and scribbles, and adding to my Authoritative Knowledge System any highlighted sections that I come across. A bit tedious, and none of these devices have had a very good or fluid UX for going through highlights. It's either some kind of index UX that lists them all and subtracts all their context, or it's one where I press a "next" button to go from highlight to highlight, which is slow and tedious. Also, for highlights, I'd often forget exactly what I was thinking when I highlighted something.

Now, when I encounter something I want to note down from a book, my system is simply jotting down a page number, and my thoughts, in whatever notebook I have at hand. This is made much easier by my recent note-taking radicalization by Sönke Ahrens in his book [How to Take Smart Notes](https://www.soenkeahrens.de/en/takesmartnotes). Namely, I now very rarely take down quotes wholesale, nor am I taking hyper-granular summaries of everything I read. Instead, I take single-idea notes, something like "on page x it says y," getting just the gist of whatever it is I read that interested me, and being highly selective to only note down things that are relevant to me. So, it's small enough that on the train I can just quickly pull out my pocket notebook, scribble out the note, put away my notebook, and go back to reading.

Another advantage is that when it's time to input notes into my Authoritative Knowledge System, I'm just flipping through pages in my notebook and ripping them out when I'm done, rather than needing to flip through a bunch of different books in my e-reader (since I'm often reading a non-fiction book and fiction book at once, taking notes on both), as well as Google Keep or wherever else. 

## It's Better than Your Laptop

But only just. By this I mean situations where you transcribe what you're hearing directly into some kind of note taking system on your laptop. Mostly: meetings, classes. I don't mean using pen and paper in lieu of a digital note organization system at all, which I address [below](#authoritative-knowledge-system). 

Reason the first, laptops, like phones, can be distracting. Email is but a click away. I distinctly remember, back when I was doing agency work for Google, in nearly every single meeting with Googlers, at some point I'd catch them checking their email. I think they have so many meetings every day, it's the only chance they get to check their email. Personally during online meetings I struggle to avoid the siren song of Hacker News or Reddit, just a tab away. 

Second, laptops are loud. If you're in class, fuck you and your gaming laptop's 120000rpm fan with clacky keys. Also oops your Youtube video autoplayed at full volume when you opened the lid. Mostly facetious here, but I am still scarred from college, and every interview I've ever been in where the interviewer clacked away randomly during our conversation. 

Third, the dichotomy problem: data input into a laptop can't be input quickly and also filed correctly into your Authoritative Knowledge System, there inevitably will need to be a processing step. 

Fourth, you rob yourself of a valuable opportunity if said processing is copy/pasting. This is again Sönke Ahrens talking, as well as various other gurus I've read. I remember an interesting tidbit I picked up once: at the end of every meeting, work with everyone to record down every actionable item resulting from said meeting. Another: after every meeting, personally write down everything you can recall that's worth writing down. The premise is that not everything deserves to be recorded. If, through the course of your meeting, information is generated that someone else needs to know, the best way to give them that information isn't to give them a transcription of the meeting. That would be like dumping the raw footage of a 3 hour podcast recording, unedited. Cut for content. Note down only the most important information, similar to Ahren's recommendations for taking notes on books: the gist, the relevant information, recorded as single ideas. The easiest way to limit yourself to being able to do only that is pen and paper.

Laptop note takers (me, for years), especially the very fast typers, tend to transcribe. That's split focus, it's very difficult to get down what's being said while processing it, which means you're just gonna process it later. Instead, listen, engage with what's being said, and then take a second to note down the gist, or the action item, or nothing at all because there's still more to discuss first. The fact that the discussion happened is basically irrelevant information, as is the courses the discussion took; all those wonderfully useless bullet points the 10 duplicated fireflies.ai instances in the meeting love to email to you after. That's the conversation, which doesn't need to happen again; what you should walk out of the meeting with is just whatever useful data you need on a piece of paper to then input to your Authoritative Knowledge System (making JIRA tickets, sending calendar events, updating Wikis, whatever).

The same for class: single idea, the gist of the lecture only. You're going to need to review it either way, and the recall phase is apparently very important, according to [people smarter than me](https://www.coursera.org/learn/learning-how-to-learn).

Fifth, if the meeting is boring, you can doodle in your notebook, and unlike the email-checkers, you'll appear very attentive and engaged.

## It's Better than Your TODO System

So, to clarify, I mean your TODO system *for today*. I think it would probably be hell to try to maintain a full TODO system on paper only. That said, I have enjoyed semi-duplicating my TODO system to a [Hobonichi](https://www.1101.com/store/techo/en/). 

My system is a [cascading TODO system in Trilium](posts/my-new-life-stack/#task-management). Basically, year-month-week-day tasks, all being shuffled around. Trilium remains the source of truth, but moment to moment I interact with the front page of the small notebook I carry around - the back pages are for random thoughts or book notes. (I stole this idea from a Hacker News comment I've repeatedly failed to find. Sorry stranger, I've stolen your idea without attribution.) 

I start each day by writing the day and date at the top, which is helpful for me throughout the day because I often forget both, and it also helps me be somewhat  aware of time going by, how far into the year or month I am. The next line is always the TODO item, "Start daily list," which I immediately cross out. This is to trick my sloppy human brain into remembering that crossing out TODOs is fun and gives the good dopamine. Then, I write down everything I'm already worried about for the day that can be translated into a TODO, which could be as simple as "look up xyz" or "message abc." Then, when I get a chance, I'm at a computer working in my Authoritative Knowledge System, copying down any daily TODOs it presents to me into my notebook. At the end of the day, I'll reconcile the two, updating the Authoritative Knowledge System as needed. Usually at this point my notebook has collected some new TODOs as well, that might get put into the next Day TODOs, or Week or Month TODOs.

I've tried really cool TODO integrations with Orgzly on my phone, or Todoist; one time I even had this really slick setup using VTODO entries against a self-hosted Davical calendar. However, the dichotomy issue again: All of these systems, when mobile, had interaction friction, and none were as good as the desktop experience. So, I've separated ingest and daily TODO handling from my overall main TODO system, which like any proper procrastinator's system, is incredibly complicated and awesome. Pen and paper meanwhile has no friction. This is especially important for my ADHD brain: my fiance will ask me to take out the trash, and if I don't get that TODO down within seconds, there's a solid chance I'll completely forget it. Reducing friction is also really helpful in allowing me to quickly jot out anything that's worrying me in even the slightest, which is again a very important technique for ADHD management: once it's on paper, it's one less thing hogging my very limited resources in the form of short term memory. It's on the ingest system, it's safe, I can worry about it when it's time to do the thing, and it took me two seconds to get it in there.

This method also has a very fun tactile element to it, wherein I cross out TODOs I complete. I used to scoff at these sorts of silly little motivational tricks, but one thing I've been learning as I get older is that I'm not special, I'm just a sloppy-brained human, and sloppy-brained human tricks work as well on me as anyone else. So, I don't stoically fight these tricks anymore to make myself "immune" to them (which is at worst impossible and at best a waste of energy), I instead leverage these tricks as shortcuts. It feels good to scratch out the TODO, it gives me a little dopamine hit. I'll take those hits where I can get them. Furthermore, I get to rip the page out at the end of the day and put it in my jar (see below), which is also quite satisfying.


## Authoritative Knowledge System

All of my previous solutions were still halfway solutions. All systems suffer the same dichotomy as the phone app: it can be immediate, or it can fit properly into my Authoritative Knowledge System, but it can't be both, because for it to fit well into my Authoritative Knowledge System, it needs to be tagged, linked, and possibly put in a certain folder (or turned into a calendar event). So, why use a system that was kinda better than pen and paper in some ways, such as being automatically backed up or having searchability, but still needed to be fed into my Authoritative Knowledge System anyway? Why not just use the simplest tool, always?

So, I use pen and paper for basically everything, and then ingest things into my Authoritative Knowledge System. By Authoritative Knowledge System, I don't just mean Personal Knowledge Management tools like Obsidian or org-mode, I mean the entire digital system that acts as the authoritative source of truth for my life. For me that's Trilium, my calendar (self hosted, because of course), and [Monica](https://www.monicahq.com/) (a new experiment). It could also mean my [co-op's](https://508.dev) ERP or some other CRM or project management tool I'm using for a project involving other people. My goal is to get anything from my ingest (pen and paper) into that system within a day of recording it, then immediately discarding that paper forever. Of course, I'm an obsessive data hoarder, so by discarding I mean I just put it somewhere where I don't need to think about it ever again: I chuck the notes into a jar on my desk that I empty once a year.

I have a tendency to use organization and systems tweaking as a means of procrastination, and keeping the system simple removes a lot of variables for me to waste time playing with. I may spend a lot of time tweaking my Trilium (or org or whatever) system, but in the end it typically serves a good purpose, since I use these systems to drive blog posts, or to review notes before another therapy session, or to find out when who said what at some meeting. 

Ingest: fast, low-friction, cheap, disposable. Digest: Robust, well-organized, high-friction, high-usability, backed up, flexible.
