I had a bizarre conversation a few months ago with an ultra-libertarian (in the
American sense). I was talking with him about how Taiwan's Youbike system is
subsidized by the government such that the first 30 minutes of each ride is
free, which is enormously useful for the city insomuch as it reduces traffic,
reduces emissions, and essentially solves the last-mile problem for the city.

He didn't like it. He didn't think it was acceptable that car drivers "had to
pay" for bike riders (through taxes). I pointed out that everyone in the city
pays for, for example, roads that are only really for car drivers, which is good
for us since e.g. delivery trucks use it, or generally speaking, a functional
road system enables commerce. He said we could have that with private, pay to
use roads. I asked, what about the obvious drawbacks of such a system, such as
such a system reinforcing a wealth divide, since poor people won't be able to
afford to use the road as much and are thus restricted from participating in
normal life as much? His answer: _"I don't think we should value all people
equally."_

I was at first at a loss for words, despite being pretty sure I would reach a
point like this with him eventually: usually I can carry a conversation down to
the roots of a fundamental personal values incompatibility with someone (there's
that word again!). I managed to figure out that what he meant wasn't quite as
abhorrent as I initially thought. He wasn't suggesting that some humans have
less moral worth than others, rather he meant that he cares more about his
family members than some random people at the end of a dirt road. I didn't get a
chance to explore with him what a society looks like when everyone thinks so
myopically, or explore with him why he arbitrarily draws the line of caring
about either people at family, or perhaps friends - how far? What about your
neighbors? Your fellow fans of a given sports team? People who share your hobby,
or ideology? People who vote like you do? Why not extend to your neighborhood,
city, state, country? Anyway, I've been thinking about this, and I think we've
overloaded the word "Value," and I think many people take advantage of this fact
to engage in rhetorical tricks.

I'm aware of four possible meanings of the word "value" when used to describe
"valuing someone:"

1. Moral worth: your belief whether a given human has equal standing with other
   humans by virtue of their being human
2. Personal attachment: your emotional connection to someone based on proximity,
   genetics, or other factors
3. Instrumental value: your belief in the utilitarian usefulness of someone; how
   much profit they can generate, how much labor they can provide, how much they
   can innovate
4. Allocation priority: how much of a scarce resource should be assigned to
   them, how resources are distributed in a social system
