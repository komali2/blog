Instagram buzzes your phone and re-displays a message you received several days
ago that you haven't replied to yet. When you unlock your phone and tap the
notification, instead of taking you to the message directly, it takes you to
your feed. At the top of it is something that grabs your attention, like a funny
comic or a half naked person. You habitually scroll to grab another dopamine hit
of content. The next post is an advertisement with an AI generated image that
you find oddly compelling, and the one after that is a political influencer
pointing out that that politician you hate did another awful thing.

We're under psychic attack. The purpose of the assault is to commoditize our
existence, so as to increase shareholder value. Corporations are
profit-generating algorithms, and like any good algorithm with no termination
criteria, they'll go happily runaway. Don't worry about
[paperclips](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/ai-and-paperclip-problem), we
already have artificial group intelligences optimizing the generation of profit.

Humans can't be told what to do, we're
[famously very stubborn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief_perseverance).
That's fine, ask Claude "find a way to convince a population to make us money -
don't make mistakes" and it'll happily loop into the solution that Instagram
did: You're more than willing to provide unlimited training data to Instagram,
as well as eyeballs for its advertisers, in return for ragebait and FOMO pics
that Instagram itself doesn't even need to generate.

I'm beginning to feel more and more lately that the most powerful thing a person
can do is develop their rhetorical ability. By rhetoric, I mean, the behaviors,
styles, words, and strategies people implement so as to influence, convince,
convert, and generally modify the behavior of other humans.

Excellent rhetoricians have achieved the incredible:
[Charles Manson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Manson) convinced a group
of kids to murder nine people.
[Jim Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonestown#Deaths_in_Jonestown)
convinced 900 people to kill themselves - yes, some at gunpoint, but he also
convinced those holding the guns to also kill themselves. I think it's fair to
assume that figures like Jesus of Nazareth or Muhammad were skilled
rhetoricians, and look what that skill can earn you: millennia-old religions.

What does it take to convince people to die en masse, or believe in new gods, or
subject themselves to pain and torment for said new gods? What does it take to
convince people to rise in revolution? To march thousands of miles in the snow?
Is it a pattern of speech? A way of looking at people? A specific combination of
words? Tone of voice? A style of dress, hair? A well-formed strategy of public
appearances?

Neal Stephenson has written several times about the seemingly magical power of
words, and what it means when machines start learning how to leverage that
power. In "Snow Crash," a virus is ravaging the internet. Its form is merely a
bitmap that, when viewed, sends the viewer into a coma. It's suggested that this
is an ancient attack vector re-discovered, a Sumerian "Nam-Shub":

> "A nam-shub is a speech with magical force. The closest English equivalent
> would be 'incantation,' but this has a number of incorrect connotations."
>
> "Did the Sumerians believe in magic?"
>
> The Librarian shakes his head minutely. “This is the kind of seemingly precise
> question that is in fact very profound, and that pieces of software, such as
> myself, are notoriously clumsy at. Allow me to quote from Kramer, Samuel Noah,
> and Maier, John R. Myths of Enki, the Crafty God. New York, Oxford: Oxford
> University Press, 1989: ‘Religion, magic, and medicine are so completely
> intertwined in Mesopotamia that separating them is frustrating and perhaps
> futile work.... [Sumerian incantations] demonstrate an intimate connection
> between the religious, the magical, and the esthetic so complete that any
> attempt to pull one away from the other will distort the whole.'

Later, in "Fall, or Dodge in Hell," he explores the possibilities of this power
if AI is applied to it:

> “Do they ever cross the river?”

> “When they get desperate or some meme convinces them it’s a good idea. They
> follow these weird edit streams. No one knows where they come from. I looked
> at one of them once. I thought it would be conspiracy-theory stuff but it
> wasn't even coherent enough to be called that.”

> “Hmm. Yeah, I guess the whole point of a conspiracy theory is to offer a kind
> of false coherence.”

> “That’s right... but what I saw didn't even rise to that standard—didn't even
> know about it. It was—well, just plain weird. Algorithmically generated
> mishmash images, sounds . . . no sense to it at all. Just whatever worked, you
> know, in the sense of getting the viewer to watch a little more. They use eye
> tracking, you don’t even have to click. But every so often, whoever’s behind
> it—whoever generates these edit streams, assuming, that is, that there’s a
> human anywhere in that loop—will put it into the minds of those people, and
> then bad things can happen.”

Of course, the idea of algorithmically generated images finding _just_ the right
combination of colors to completely reprogram people is a bit silly. I hesitate
to completely dismiss the general idea, though, given
[Elsagate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elsagate)

Elsagate was a phenomenon involving of a lot of _very weird_ Youtube videos. A
group of creators had been experimenting with various video formats, and one day
a couple of them threw on 5$ Halloween costumes of Elsa, Spiderman, and other
popular characters, and then acted out a bunch of slapstick scenarios. The video
blew up on Youtube Kids, and so they, operating as a views-generating group mind
/ algorithm, pumped out more of these videos.

Inevitably, other channels jumped on the moneywagon, thus creating the "Elsa
Spiderman" genre of video. Relatively harmless at first, but eventually these
views-generating algorithms needed to stand out from the crowd, as the space got
flooded and overall views went down through sheer supply saturation. At that
point the videos started getting weird. Frequent themes would includes
pregnancy, pooping, and simulated sex, because videos featuring this got more
views and better view times. Why? What about Elsa fake pooping out plastic balls
enthralls children of all cultures? Is that a real-life example of a Nan-Shub?
Towards the end of the saga, semi-automated animation studios had been plugged
into the genre, and the speed at which shit got freaky went exponential.

I read a random reddit comment as it all went down that described the Elsagate
videos, and the characters within them, as "Psychofauna we don't understand,
feeding on our children's minds."

I've been wondering if this will keep happening now that image and text
generation AIs have become widespread. Apparently,
[boomers are going coo-coo](https://restofworld.org/2026/china-seniors-ai-slop/)
for cocoslop. Something about the form of them seems to draw them in and hold
their attention, moreso than non-generated images. Is it hard to believe that
algorithmic generation loops would stumble upon combinations that reach deep
into our brains and enthrall us?

That's of course a far cry from actually influencing our behavior. Cory Doctorow
is skeptical of this theorized magical power that machine learning has given the
big surveillance companies like Facebook. He describes the mystical stories of
ML ad-targeting as just another aspect of advertising - of course Facebook wants
people to believe their feed algorithm is near magical, because then advertisers
are willing to spend more to get on it!
[He writes:](https://pluralistic.net/2023/11/03/subprime-attention-rent-crisis/)

> a platform's job is to make these connections, and that means platforms are
> always in the algorithm business... the platform has to make a call as to what
> you're going to see or do.
>
> These choices are enormously consequential. In the theory of Surveillance
> Capitalism, these choices take on an almost supernatural quality, where "Big
> Data" can be used to guess your response to all the different ways of pitching
> an idea or product to you, in order to select the optimal pitch that bypasses
> your critical faculties and actually controls your actions, robbing you of
> "the right to a future tense."
>
> I don't think much of this hypothesis. Every claim to mind control – from
> Rasputin to MK Ultra to neurolinguistic programming to pick-up artists – has
> turned out to be bullshit. Besides, you don't need to believe in mind control
> to explain the ways that algorithms shape our beliefs and actions. When a
> single company dominates the information landscape – say, when Google controls
> 90% of your searches – then Google's sorting can deprive you of access to
> information without you knowing it.

I share his skepticism at least insomuch as I don't trust platforms' claims
about their own algorithm, but I do believe that we're being beset upon by
psychological paperclip factories that we as individual humans don't have
techniques to resist. At minimum, the constant assault is reducing our quality
of life. At worse, it's driving our entire existence - convincing us to
participate in excessive consumerism, driving us to hate our fellow countrymen
or people we don't know and never will meet in other countries, or
[leading us to suicide](https://news.sky.com/story/molly-russell-inquest-teenager-died-by-self-harm-while-suffering-negative-effects-of-online-content-coroner-finds-12707322).
It's also causing us to turn our existence towards the purpose of platform
profit generation: FOMO causes us to take more pictures that we upload to
Instagram for AI training while at the same time increasing the value of the
platform by adding to the amount of content on it, while _at the same time_
being advertised to! Every post on Reddit helps the Reddit sales pitch to
advertisers and LLM companies to whom they sell your post for further training.
Every Tweet, eaten by Grok, making a trillionaire even more billions.

There's pushback. There's an "analog" movement of people opting into dumbphones,
or abandoning streaming services for MP3 players, or switching to e-ink
notetaking devices to try to get themselves away from the distraction of their
computers. Of course, capitalism noticed, and has infested the scene with
consumerism. The message of "try this dumbphone out to help you avoid
doomscrolling" has turned into "you NEED to buy this to fix your social media
addiction! You can't do it on your own, but this one last purchase will be the
thing that finally solves it." Inevitable. Always the cycles: hook you on the
dopamine loop, then sell you what you need to get unhooked; but of course, the
medicine doesn't _actually_ work. I'm reminded of the fact that antidepressants
are some of the most profitable medicines to sell, since they don't actually
_cure_ anything, they simply are a life-long treatment for a disease that
[they might not even be effectively treating](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-022-01661-0).
(That's not a call to stop taking your psychiatric meds; listen to your doctors,
kids)

I want to believe we have all the tools we need today to save ourselves from
social media FOMO, doomscrolling, and dopamine holes, in the form of
"willpower," but experience is teaching me the importance of structural effects
in managing my ADHD. If leaving my gym shorts next to my bed makes all the
difference between me going to the gym or not in the morning, maybe it's true
that we just need to have a phone that can't launch Instagram at all. If nothing
else, it seems that
[willpower is a limited resource](https://www.apa.org/topics/willpower-limited.pdf),
and the need to constantly spend it every time we look at our phones is draining
our ability to apply it to other situations throughout the day. For me, the
application of bog-standard addiction management techniques
[helped me break my addiction to reddit](/posts/how-to-quit-social-media), so
maybe this can be generalized to the population?

Or, perhaps activists can fight fire with fire. The documentary filmmaker
[Kalle Lasn](https://inquiringmind.com/article/1901_7_lasn_1-cleaning-up-the-mental-environment-culture-jamming-with-kalle-lasn/),
known for launching campaigns such as "Buy Nothing Day," tried this strategy. He
created "anti-ads," or "mind-bombs," such as an ad that simply describes that
"over 50% of the calories from a Big Mac come from fat." An obvious issue stems
from the fact that we don't own the platforms. When Lasn tried this, not only
was it expensive, the major networks eventually simply refused to show his ads.
In Canada this is illegal, but that's no matter, the Supreme Court of Canada
simply refused to hear his case, thus solving any anxiety from the networks.

[Micah Bornfree](https://micahmwhite.com/adbusters-articles/toxic-culture-a-unified-theory-of-mental-pollution)
characterizes the psychic assault as "mental pollution," and hopes to use this
framing to allow for the same techniques governments use to wrangle corporate
environmental pollution, to also wrangle mental pollution (I believe the idea
originates with Michel Serres). I like his characterization, especially of how
corporations use pollution as a weapon:

> The Earth is being claimed by corporations. Whether it is by appropriating the
> ocean with their oil spills or by seizing public spaces with their
> advertisements, corporations use hard and soft pollution to steal what is
> ours.

I'm skeptical, though, because it's not like we're doing a great job at
preventing corporations from setting the world on fire, especially during the AI
era. The needs of the Datacenter overrule the petty needs of the humans.

## Solution?

One of my favorite things that happened this decade was when Musk started
selling Twitter verification badges, and people immediately used these badges to
impersonate several large companies, for example posting from an impersonated
Lockheed Martin account that the company would no longer be selling weapons to
Israel, thus wiping
[billions off their stock value](https://www.eurasiantimes.com/no-weapons-sales-to-israel-how-a-lockheed-martin-tweet-resulted/)
within minutes. That's a BEAUTIFUL form of direct action: low effort, cheap,
able to be performed by basically any individual anywhere, and enormously
impactful. With LLMs, I wonder if a similar opportunity exists for direct
action.

In the aforementioned "Fall, or Dodge in Hell," the memetic, chaotic, and
useless form of the internet was caused a decade earlier in the book by a
character that released a series of FOSS bots that anyone could deploy that
could, given a topic or person, seed an insurmountable amount of disinformation
about said topic or person, thus making it impossible to separate fact from
fiction. I don't like the idea of destroying the entire internet, but I do like
the idea of nuking platform value. Perhaps there's some way to leverage LLMs to
fill the platforms with so much useless, boring - yet just plausible enough to
get past the algorithm - noise, that people close Instagram in disgust as it
fails to deliver the ever-promised dopamine hit?

Doctorow is probably right that Facebook isn't casting magic spells on us, but
there is _something_ new happening here. The entire psychology-of-advertising
industry exists around the premise that there are as of yet undiscovered
techniques to get people to buy your product instead of your competitors', or to
look at your social media app instead of the other one, or to click your ads
first. As these people throw Machine Learning at the problem, and overwhelm us
with the results of their studies, what have we been doing to resist this? Is
there a psychological equivalent to a suit of armor that we can don to reduce
the effect of the assault on our minds? Is there a memetic equivalent of a
healing potion, something we can create for ourselves to "reset us to baseline"
at the end of long day of being psychic assault?

Practically speaking, we can redesign our environment, of course. We could use
dumbphones, put our phones in black and white mode, uninstall Instagram, install
ad blockers, put [pi-holes](https://pi-hole.net/) on our network to block
unwanted content. Somehow this feels more like hiding, though. I wonder if
there's something we can do to face the assault head-on, something to regain
control of our willpower and free will.
